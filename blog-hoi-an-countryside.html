Cycling Hội An's Countryside: What's Actually Out There

Ten minutes past the Old Town, the tourists stop and the rice starts.

Hội An's Ancient Town is about two kilometres across. Most visitors spend three days inside that square, and then leave thinking they've seen Hội An. What they've seen is the part that's been preserved for them.

Ride ten minutes in almost any direction and it changes completely. No souvenir shops, no tailors, no menus in four languages. Just rice fields, water buffalo, people working, and roads with almost nobody on them.

Why bikes work here

The land around Hội An is completely flat — it's a river delta. There are no hills to fight, and the back roads between villages are narrow concrete paths with very little traffic. You're not sharing a highway with trucks; you're on a lane wide enough for one motorbike, passing houses where someone will almost certainly wave at you.

It's also the right speed. In a car you see the countryside through glass at 50km/h. On a bike you smell the rice, hear the birds, and can stop whenever something looks interesting.

The craft villages

The area around Hội An still has working craft villages — not demonstration villages built for tourists, but places where people actually make things for a living.

Mat weaving

Sleeping mats woven from rush grass, dyed in bright colours, made on wooden looms on the floor. It takes two people: one feeding the reeds through, one working the loom. Families here have done it for generations, and they'll usually let you sit down and try — which is when you find out how hard it is to keep the tension even.

Noodle making

Rice noodles made by hand, spread out on bamboo racks to dry in the sun. It's a whole morning's work for what ends up as a few bowls of noodles.

Wood carving

The carving tradition around Hội An goes back centuries — a lot of what you see in the Old Town's old houses came from workshops in these villages.

The coconut basket boat

The thuyền thúng is a round bamboo boat, sealed and waterproofed, that looks like it shouldn't float and definitely shouldn't be steerable. Fishermen have used them here for generations — the story is that they were built round to get around a French tax on boats.

You take one out through the water coconut palms on the Thu Bồn river. The boatmen can spin them in tight circles with a single oar, which looks impossible right up until they do it.

What to expect

  • Flat riding, no hills, suitable for anyone who can ride a bike.
  • Heat is the main challenge, not distance — mornings are much better than midday.
  • Rain happens, especially October to December. A thin poncho handles it and the fields look better wet.
  • Bring sunscreen and water. There's shade in the villages but not much between them.
  • What you see depends on the day. Farmers work to their own schedule, not a tour schedule — sometimes you'll hit a field being planted, sometimes harvested, sometimes empty.

The point of it

This isn't a highlights tour. There's no single famous thing to photograph and cross off. It's a few hours of seeing how people around Hội An actually live — which, for a lot of travellers, ends up being the part of the trip they talk about afterwards.
